Marine ecosystems provide essential ecosystem services such as the provisioning of food or the regulation of climate through controlling the biogeochemical cycling of, e.g., carbon, nitrogen or sulphur. However, ecosystem functioning is under threat from direct or indirect human influences (climate change, pollution, fishing, tourism). Marine ecosystem modeling is a critical tool to anticipate how these systems may respond to cumulative pressures. Although marine ecosystem models must simplify immense biological complexity to remain computationally feasible, advances in observation-based understanding of marine ecosystem functioning require their continuous refinement. **Our group aims to enhance the representation of biological processes in ocean models to improve our understanding of how marine organisms influence biogeochemical cycles and how marine ecosystems are, in turn, influenced by the environment surrounding them.** To achieve that, our group adapts model parametrizations or adds biological complexity to ocean models. After thorough evaluation with existing observational datasets, model simulations under present and future climates reveal how marine ecosystem functioning and ecosystem services may evolve in the future. 


## Related Projects

[PHYVIR](https://www.nwo.nl/en/projects/afomm75533) (2025-2030): Phytoplankton (tiny ocean plants) produce half the Earth’s oxygen and feed the seas, but are infected by viruses. Although we have all experienced the massive effect viruses can have at local and global scales, very little is still known about their impact on phytoplankton in the oceans. This project aims to fill this knowledge gap, by investigating virus-phytoplankton interactions in the laboratory and during oceanic expeditions, and by building advanced computer models to predict how marine viruses affect carbon and nutrient cycling in the ocean and, thus, our planet's health. This project is funded by the Dutch Research Council (NWO) and led by Corina Brussaard (NIOZ). Cara Nissen (University of Amsterdam) is one of the co-leads. 

[C-CONNECT](https://www.nwo.nl/en/projects/phwfd50803) (2026-2031): Our climate is to large extent mediated by storing and recycling carbon in our ocean through the so-called “biological pump”, especially at the poles. The Arctic pump needs serious plumbing though! Some important parts that greatly influence the cycling of carbon, like swimming snails and animals that live at the deep, dark, ocean floor, are not yet connected to the pump. In nature they are connected, but they are not yet accounted for in climate change calculations. This project will determine carbon flows by these missing pump components to better predict how our climate will change. This project is funded by the Dutch Research Council (NWO)  and led by Jasper de Goeij (University of Amsterdam), with co-leads Cara Nissen (University of Amsterdam), Katja Peijnenburg (Naturalis/University of Amsterdam) and Furu Mienis (NIOZ).
